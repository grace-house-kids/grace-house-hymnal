If Love Was a Rule
If Love Was a Rule
[Date: From the before]
If love were a rule, I could only ever truly love myself. 
If love were a rule, people would stop being people and start being an occasion.
If love were a rule, I would be loving the version of me who loved Him.
If love were a rule, I would resent the person who cost me more of it. 
If love were a rule, every hard person in my life would be a bill to pay.
If love were a rule, I could never actually see you. 
But love is not a rule. 
Love is what spills out of a person who has finally seen how loved they are.
That person can look up from the ledger because the ledger is closed. 
Paid. 
Nailed to a cross. 
That person can love you because they’re no longer trying to save themselves by doing it.
The Law died so that love could live.

The Height of a Coffin
The Height of a Coffin
[Date: From the before]
There is a country whose light falls without shadow because it is everywhere at once, and everything in that country is as it is because there is nothing there that can hide. And through that country runs a road, and on the road stands a man.
He came through a gate. He remembers standing before it and seeing that it was the width of a needle's eye and the height of a coffin and that he could not fit through it. 
Now he stands on a road and looks back through the gate.
The gate from this side is not what it was from the other. From the other side it had been terrible, impassable. From this side it is beautiful. And through it he can see the country he came from, and far off, he can see his brother.
His brother is standing in a field and around him are papers. Some are rolled in satchels and some in his hands as he walks in slow, deliberate circles. He is making a map. He has been for years. In the corner of that map, drawn with great care, is a gate. It is a fine gate, hidden enough and broad enough for a man of his intellect and stature to walk through upright.
But it is not the gate he is looking for. That gate is behind him, small and low, and he has never turned to face it, because when he first came into the field and glimpsed it out of the corner of his eye he registered at once that no man could fit through such a passage.The true gate, he reasoned, must lie elsewhere. 
The brother crosses paths with another man who carries a map as well. He has many men following him. He is a master cartographer. But it is a different map, drawn on different paper. 
The two men see each other and stop. They greet each other cordially but as they compare their maps, the men begin to argue. They argue about the meaning of certain hills. They argue about what the ancient descriptions of the gate should be taken to imply about its dimensions. They argue about whether the gate is more likely to be found at the end of a difficult path or at the end of an obvious one. 
They will argue in the valley for a long time. Perhaps forever. Neither of their maps leads anywhere. Both were drawn at a distance from the only gate, by men who saw that gate and turned away because they could not accept its dimensions. 
The maps are elaborate. They seem internally consistent. They are the work of intelligent and sincere men. And they are drawings of nothing. The country they are meant to guide toward does not lie at the end of either route, because the country cannot be reached by any route, only by a gate that neither map contains, and the gate is behind both of them, in a field one of them left hours ago and the other left years ago, and neither is going back to.
The man on the road, in the country of unshadowed light, watches all of this and he turns from the gate. He does not turn away in the sense of abandoning his brother. He turns because there is a road, and the road goes somewhere, and he is being drawn along it by the light that is everywhere in that country, and he walks.
Behind him, in the valley, the two men are still arguing. They will construct, between them, over the years, an entire literature of the argument. They will write treatises defending their respective maps. They will develop schools of thought around them. They will train students in their methods, and their students will refine the maps further, and further schools will develop, and further arguments, and libraries will be built to house the treatises, and the libraries will be grand, and inside them scholars will pursue the location of the gate with tremendous rigor and sincerity, but none of them will find it.
The gate is small. It is low. It is the width of a needle's eye and the height of a coffin.
