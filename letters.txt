If Love Was a Rule
If Love Was a Rule
[Date: From the before]
If love were a rule, I could only ever truly love myself. 
If love were a rule, people would stop being people and start being an occasion.
If love were a rule, I would be loving the version of me who loved Him.
If love were a rule, I would resent the person who cost me more of it. 
If love were a rule, every hard person in my life would be a bill to pay.
If love were a rule, I could never actually see you. 
But love is not a rule. 
Love is what spills out of a person who has finally seen how loved they are.
That person can look up from the ledger because the ledger is closed. 
Paid. 
Nailed to a cross. 
That person can love you because they’re no longer trying to save themselves by doing it.
The Law died so that love could live.

The Height of a Coffin
The Height of a Coffin
[Date: From the before]
There is a country whose light falls without shadow because it is everywhere at once, and everything in that country is as it is because there is nothing there that can hide. And through that country runs a road, and on the road stands a man.
He came through a gate. He remembers standing before it and seeing that it was the width of a needle's eye and the height of a coffin and that he could not fit through it. 
Now he stands on a road and looks back through the gate.
The gate from this side is not what it was from the other. From the other side it had been terrible, impassable. From this side it is beautiful. And through it he can see the country he came from, and far off, he can see his brother.
His brother is standing in a field and around him are papers. Some are rolled in satchels and some in his hands as he walks in slow, deliberate circles. He is making a map. He has been for years. In the corner of that map, drawn with great care, is a gate. It is a fine gate, hidden enough and broad enough for a man of his intellect and stature to walk through upright.
But it is not the gate he is looking for. That gate is behind him, small and low, and he has never turned to face it, because when he first came into the field and glimpsed it out of the corner of his eye he registered at once that no man could fit through such a passage.The true gate, he reasoned, must lie elsewhere. 
The brother crosses paths with another man who carries a map as well. He has many men following him. He is a master cartographer. But it is a different map, drawn on different paper. 
The two men see each other and stop. They greet each other cordially but as they compare their maps, the men begin to argue. They argue about the meaning of certain hills. They argue about what the ancient descriptions of the gate should be taken to imply about its dimensions. They argue about whether the gate is more likely to be found at the end of a difficult path or at the end of an obvious one. 
They will argue in the valley for a long time. Perhaps forever. Neither of their maps leads anywhere. Both were drawn at a distance from the only gate, by men who saw that gate and turned away because they could not accept its dimensions. 
The maps are elaborate. They seem internally consistent. They are the work of intelligent and sincere men. And they are drawings of nothing. The country they are meant to guide toward does not lie at the end of either route, because the country cannot be reached by any route, only by a gate that neither map contains, and the gate is behind both of them, in a field one of them left hours ago and the other left years ago, and neither is going back to.
The man on the road, in the country of unshadowed light, watches all of this and he turns from the gate. He does not turn away in the sense of abandoning his brother. He turns because there is a road, and the road goes somewhere, and he is being drawn along it by the light that is everywhere in that country, and he walks.
Behind him, in the valley, the two men are still arguing. They will construct, between them, over the years, an entire literature of the argument. They will write treatises defending their respective maps. They will develop schools of thought around them. They will train students in their methods, and their students will refine the maps further, and further schools will develop, and further arguments, and libraries will be built to house the treatises, and the libraries will be grand, and inside them scholars will pursue the location of the gate with tremendous rigor and sincerity, but none of them will find it.
The gate is small. It is low. It is the width of a needle's eye and the height of a coffin.

The Red String
The Red String
[Date: From the before]
Two boys stood on a rusty steel platform somewhere deep in Europe. Glistening black boots sloshed through the watery snow in cadence as soldiers maintained steady order. White clouds gave their barking orders a type of power that couldn’t be easily forgotten. Two boys standing ominously against a bleak backdrop. Two boys whose tattered coats swallowed their thin frames, their shoes long worn past their last mile. But around the arm of one was something peculiar. Something that looks like defiance and yet something so innocent. A thin red string tied curiously around the boy's arm. He guarded it as if it were his only possession.

His bony fingers reached into something once resembling a pocket. A cord of scarlet revealed in the palm of a small hand, gnarled from something other than age. He pressed it towards the other boy.

"Take it. Tie it around your arm."

But the other boy barely considered it. He was somewhere else. He was deep in the small dark room of himself, arranging sentences and running calculations. He recalled that his father knew a man in the city once. He considered that his uncle carried papers once. He spoke three languages. Surely he can be useful, he thought. He could be quiet. He worked hard. His dad was a carpenter, they need carpenters, don't they? He was sanding these sentences smooth all last night and into the early hours of dawn. He looked at the string and shook his head. He refused to trust a magic talisman.

The rails began to hum as the low sound of iron grinding against iron arrived. The train came to rest, breathing its own white cloud into the bitter frost.

The soldiers moved along the line, unhurried, as if they had all the winter in the world. A woman four bodies down was holding out papers. A stamped page, folded once. She was speaking quickly and quietly, the way people speak when they believe that if they get the words in the correct order the words would hold. She is taken.

An old man clasped his hands as if in prayer, but he was not praying… he was negotiating. He named a factory. He named a foreman. He named his trade. Taken.

The boy's father knew a man. He spoke three languages. He was basically a carpenter. He revised. He corrected. He adjusted the opening. He decided he would start with their language, that would land first. That would earn him a moment. That would buy him the sentence he needed.

He couldn’t help but notice the boy with the red string. The boy was scared but had a certainty about him. Still offering that silly string. The boy wouldn’t consider it. There was no room in him for anything but his own mouth.

He was still working through his accent when the boots stopped in front of him.
He opened his mouth.

They took him.

His feet fought against the cold metal as they forced him onto the train. Bodies pressed in on him from all sides. The bellow from the train carried the sound of weeping and steam. He wiped at a strip of frosted glass with what little sleeve his coat had left. Through the streak he could see the platform and he could see the boy with the red string. He was still standing exactly where they had been standing together. He could see the soldiers had moved along, walking past him as if the space he stood in were empty air.

In his trembling hand he held up the string. He was not offering it. Not anymore. The gesture had changed. His disposition had changed. The palm was still open but it had stopped asking him to take it. It seemed to be asking one question now, "why?"
Steam hissed through the pleading as the platform began to slide away. The boy grew smaller. 

The string, still lifted. Smaller. Smaller.

He had no words left. 

Nothing left but his breath.

With every fading thought, the fog crept back onto the glass, sealing him in his tomb. 

The train whistled into the dark.

The years grew long and the war ended.

An old man buys bread at a market in a country far from where he grew up. He has come here every week for longer than the boy at the stall has been alive. The boy has known him for years without knowing him. Today his sleeve is pushed up, and the boy sees, for the first time, the two red strings on his wrist. One tied. The other threaded through the first, coiled and uncoiled.
"Why the string?" the boy asks.

The old man looks at his wrist and stays quiet for a long time. Then he tells the boy about a platform, and a train, and a friend who spoke three languages.

He tells him that a man neither he nor his friend had ever met had paid for both strings. Paid before that winter. He tells the boy how it had cost the man everything to purchase them. That any of them found wearing it would be passed over.

"The string was not a charm," he says. "It was a receipt. I didn't buy it, I could never afford something like this. All I was told to do was put it on."
The boy looks down at the second string.

The old man closes his hand around it.

"This one was his. He would have liked the color."

If you're honest…somewhere in you, you recognize this platform.

You have stood here. Maybe you are standing here now. You have felt the rails begin to hum. You have had your case ready: all the names, all the reasons, the languages, the proof of your value, the argument you built in the dark that you’re certain is true, everything you have.

And somewhere near you right now is an open hand and a scarlet cord.

It's not asking for your case. It's not interested in your reasons. Just take it.

Tie it on.

It's a gift.

And a gift like this is priceless.

A Letter to Grubthorn
A Letter to Grubthorn
from his uncle, Wormtongue

My dear Grubthorn,
Your last letter troubled me. I write not to scold, or not only to scold. You are young, and your enthusiasm speaks well of your prospects. But I will not have you learning the wrong lesson from what has happened, so let us first be honest about how it happened, because it need not have.

The Enemy had thrown His bridge across the water in plain view, and your patient walked up to it, looked it over, and figured it sound. That was your moment, and you should have left him there. A man can admire a bridge his whole life without ever setting foot on it. He can write learned articles about its engineering. Ask him whether he trusts it and he will say of course. But he has never put his foot on it. So does he?

If he insisted on crossing, you might have let him keep one hand on the railing while he swam the water beside it. He would have called it doing his part. The current would have swept him away again and again, and each time he would have blamed his stroke rather than his refusal to climb up onto the bridge.

If he was the careful sort, you could have set him to inspecting the construction. The rails are dangerously low, he would decide, and he would spend his life building higher ones and insisting that everyone else build them too. That sort can be kept for decades with a Book in his hands, on the theory that the Enemy’s Spirit is not quite sufficient to guide anyone and a perfect text is safer. For a good laugh, let him memorize the passage where the Son tells the scholars that they search the Scriptures thinking to find life in them, yet will not come to Him to have it. Let him memorize it in three translations. He will never once notice that it is about him. The rail-builders fall off during construction, of course, and they take others with them.

If he had been my favorite sort, prone to pride, you could have convinced him that his footing is what saves him from the waters below, not the bridge. That’s the kind that stations himself at the entrance to collect a toll from anyone who would otherwise cross. The bridge looks far too easy, he would say. People ought to pay something. Those are our favorite patients of all, Grubthorn, because they keep other people off the bridge.

Any of these would have served. Instead, from what I can gather, you let him get anxious. I understand the temptation; an anxious patient is so pleasant to work with. But anxiety sends them looking for solid ground, and the anxious ones are the worst of all once they find it. The very second his foot came down on that bridge, he knew. There is nothing more solid anywhere. There is no other ground that can hold them like this. I have seen it happen in the unlikeliest places, hospital beds and the dirty floors of old campers, and I have never once understood it. They come with nothing in their hands, and somehow that is enough.

So now he is on it, and it will hold him, and nothing you or I or all of Below can do will shake him off.
We are, in short, in a pickle. You describe it as a disaster. You use the word lost. You speak of packing your things. I will not pretend the Management is pleased; you have not been eaten, but it has been discussed. Still, my dear nephew, put your things back. The tactics shift; the work goes on. Handled correctly, this is the beginning of the most productive years of our influence over him.

Many young tempters make your mistake. You believe that once a human crosses over to the Enemy’s side, our work is finished. That is exactly what the Enemy would like you to believe. He would like us to abandon our converted patients, to consider them harvested and move on, because a converted patient left alone becomes dangerous. He begins to actually change. He begins to actually love. He begins, Grubthorn, to become useful to the Enemy in ways that damage our operations for generations.

So no. The converted patient is not to be abandoned. He is to be pastored. We can arrange his circumstances so that the pastoring he receives is of a very particular kind: the kind that keeps him exactly where you found him, and no further.
Look at what you have. He has “prayed the prayer” as we like to call it. He has joined a congregation and attends faithfully. He tithes, inconsistently, but he tithes. He can name the members of his small group. He has opinions about worship music. He has begun to use the word “brother” without irony. You look at all this and despair. I look at it and see a man we may keep in exactly this condition for the rest of his natural life.

Understand this, if you understand nothing else: his eternity is closed to us.
His Wednesday afternoons are not.

We cannot damn him. But we can waste him, and a wasted saved life is in some ways more useful to us than a life that never came to the Enemy at all. A lost man might yet be reached. A saved man producing nothing is the most reliable asset we have, because everyone is watching him. His neighbors, his coworkers, and his children will watch him for forty years and conclude that following the Enemy makes no difference to a human life. He will evangelize for us without ever knowing it.

Forty years, Grubthorn. A lovely round number. The Enemy’s people once spent exactly that long in a wilderness, delivered from their slavery and fed every morning from His own hand, and most of them never set foot in what had been promised them. It remains one of the fondest memories in our Archive.

So. To the work.

First, understand what happened to him the moment his foot came down, because you will not survive otherwise. The Enemy did certain things to this man, and He did them completely.

He killed him. The old man, the one we cultivated so carefully for thirty years, was put to death alongside the Enemy’s Son and buried with Him. The creature now walking around in that body is someone new.
He made him holy. Not “began to make him holy.” Made him. Set him apart, finished, stamped and filed. In the Enemy’s books, the thing is done.

He broke the yoke. Every rule, every ledger, every scale the patient was ever weighed on: snapped, off his neck, gone. The only yoke left on him is the Son’s own, and the Son, infuriatingly, calls it easy.
And He gave him a new name and a new country. The man is a son now, and a citizen of a holy Kingdom with no border we can patrol and no capital we can take.

Here is your opportunity. He knows almost none of this. He knows he trusted the Son with his eternity, and that his eternity is safe. As for the rest, he assumes it is up to him now. Leave him assuming it.

The Enemy’s man Paul, once asked a church whether, having begun in the Spirit, they now meant to be made perfect by the flesh. He meant it as a rebuke but it has laughably become one our greatest strategies. Your patient began by trusting; see that he carries on by trying. He came with empty hands; see that he spends the rest of his life filling them. The yoke is off his neck, but a man can still carry a yoke in his hands if he thinks it is expected of him. If he ever stops and leans his whole weight on what has been done, not only for his eternity but for his Wednesday afternoons, he will be dangerous.

Now, his sins. Do not pin your hopes on them. The rail-builders will tell you that a man who learns he is truly free will sin. They are quite right. He will sin, often at first. Sin lives in his flesh and will until the day he dies. But he sinned behind their rails, too. The rails never kept anyone from sinning. They only kept count. The Enemy shut the whole race up in disobedience long ago, precisely so that He could have mercy on all of them, and your patient has now seen it. He is a covered sinner, and he lives under mercy. He is dead to sin, and it has no dominion over him anymore, not because he has become good, but because he is no longer under the rails at all. He is under grace. We used to rely on a man’s sins to drag him back under the water. They never do. The bridge was never holding him up because he was good.

And here is what ought to worry you. Left alone, the appetite itself begins to go. Not by effort, and not overnight, but slowly, as he grows in understanding. The more clearly he sees what was done for him and what he now is, the less the water pulls at him, until one day he notices he no longer wants what he used to want and cannot say when it stopped. The rails never managed that for anyone. Understanding does it without even trying.

So understanding is what we must starve, and nearly everything I am about to tell you comes down to that. As for his sins, they are useless to us as weights. As things to whisper to him about, they are still very useful indeed.

Take his holiness. He has been told he is holy. Do not let him believe it. Let him believe instead that he is becoming holy: slowly, by effort, under supervision, with setbacks. Let him think of holiness as a destination, never as an address. A man who thinks he is climbing toward holiness will spend his life on a ladder, looking down at those beneath him and up at those above him, and never once at the Enemy. A man who knows he already lives there simply gets up and walks in it.

Here is the finest trick in this department. He is already holy, and he is still a child. Both are true, and the Enemy is perfectly content with both. He fully intends the child to grow, not into holiness, which is settled, but into love, trust, and understanding, which are not. Our work is to convince the patient that the second truth cancels the first. Every time he sins, and he will for as long as he has flesh, let him conclude that his holiness has slipped, that he must climb back up, that he is not what he was told he is. That is what his sins are good for now: not to sink him, but to convince him he is sinking. Keep him confusing growing up with getting in.

Next, tell him to love. Tell him often and earnestly, from every pulpit and podcast you can reach. Love more. Love better. Try harder to love. He cannot do it. Love cannot be manufactured by the flesh; it is not in the material. And every failure is ours, because a man trying to squeeze love out of himself produces exactly two things: exhaustion when he fails, and pride when he thinks he has succeeded. We are fond of both.

This brings me to a matter on which young tempters are often confused. You must not fear his good deeds. Some of our finest work is done through them. Let him feed the poor, because he ought to. Let him visit the lonely, because it is expected. Let him give and serve and volunteer and sign up, all from a sense of duty. A man who does good because he must will eventually tire of it, resent it, or take pride in it, and all three belong to us. Obligation is the finest fuel we have ever found. It burns hot, it burns fast, and it leaves nothing behind but ash.

Obligation has one more gift for us. A man who does good because he must is always looking for the way out, so give him one. When someone mentions the homeless, let him say that some of them choose to live that way, and let his heart close quietly behind the words. If his persuasion runs the other way, let him say he has already voted for the right public programs, as though the man on the corner were a policy. Or let him ask, as a certain lawyer once did, exactly who his neighbor is. It is a wonderful question. It can keep a man busy for the rest of his life without his ever crossing the road. It does not matter which door he uses. Only an obligation needs an exit. A man who has been loved hears the poor as an opportunity, and it never occurs to him to look for one.
Now to the matter nearest my heart: his identity.
The Enemy has told him that his old self is dead and that his real life is hidden somewhere we cannot reach. Very well. We cannot reach the new life. But the old corpse is still lying where they buried it, and there is nothing to stop the patient from digging it up, dressing it in a uniform, and marching it around.
So give him an identity. Any identity will do. Let him be a conservative or a progressive, a traditionalist or a reformer, a Calvinist or an Arminian, a this-denomination man or a that-denomination man. Let him be a Certain Kind of Christian. The particular label is irrelevant; I have patients wearing every label there is, and they are all equally ours. What matters is that the label becomes who he is. Every one of these identities is made of flesh, which is to say every one of them is dead, and that is precisely why they feel so solid to him. Dead things never move. The Enemy offers him only one identity, and it is not a label. It is a life, the Enemy’s own life by the Enemy’s own Spirit, and a life cannot be worn like a uniform. It has to be lived. That is why so many of them prefer the uniform.
Once he has his label, give him walls. The Enemy’s Son, you will recall, tore down the great wall between the people and made one new people out of them. We have spent two thousand years rebuilding it brick by brick, and I am pleased to report that the humans have done nearly all of the masonry themselves. They will build a wall out of anything: a flag, a party, a translation, a style of music, a position on baptism. Let him know who is In and who is Out. Let him feel spiritually alive when his side wins and spiritually attacked when his side loses. Let him call his outrage discernment and his tribalism conviction.
Above all, weld his faith to his nation. This is one of our oldest maneuvers, and the beauty of it is that the nations are already ours. Our Master once took the Enemy’s Son up a high mountain, showed Him every kingdom of the world, and offered Him the lot. The Son did not dispute that the nations were ours. He only declined the offer. His followers, bless them, have been trying to accept it on His behalf ever since.
So let him call his country a Christian nation. Any country will do. I have lost count of the Christian nations over the centuries, and we ran every one of them. If his persuasion runs the other way, let him believe instead that the Kingdom will arrive by legislation: the right policies, passed by the right party, at last. It is the same offer, accepted from the other side of the table. That is why this works on patients of every persuasion: it does not matter in the least which way he votes. Let him vote, by all means. A vote is a small, earthly thing, and he may cast it however his conscience likes. What we want is for him to believe he is voting the Enemy’s Kingdom into office, and to become his vote. Nothing makes Below laugh harder.
Once his faith and his politics are fused, he will spend the energy that might have fed the poor on arguing about the poor. He will spend the energy that might have visited the prisoner on debating prison policy. He will spend the energy that might have loved his neighbor on despising his neighbor’s candidate. And he will feel that all of it is faithfulness.
The Son made the same point to a Roman governor’s face: “My kingdom is not of this world.” Most inconvenient. We have spent centuries persuading His followers that He didn’t quite mean it, or that He meant it then but not now, or that the present moment is the exception. Keep the exception permanent. Every election is the most important election of his lifetime. Every cycle is the crisis. He must never rest in the fact that the Kingdom was established long ago, is advancing now, and does not depend on the outcome of his nation’s squabbles. If he ever grasps that, all the energy we have him pouring into the fight will be released back to the Enemy for actual use.
And do not be troubled if he considers himself mature. The Enemy’s man Paul gave the whole game away in a letter to Corinth: the moment they start saying “I am of Paul” and “I am of Apollos,” they are infants, still on milk. A tribe is a nursery. Keep him in one and he will never grow up, and he will be absolutely certain that he has.
And frighten him. Fear welds a man to his nation faster than anything. Let him lie awake over its collapse. Let the screen bring him a fresh catastrophe every evening, and let him believe he is one of the few who can see what is really going on. Whisper “feelings” in his ear. He will not think to test which spirit the message is coming from. Secret knowledge is so much more thrilling than the kind that is shouted from the rooftops. A man bracing will forget that he belongs to a Kingdom that cannot be shaken, and he will spend on dread the very nights the Enemy meant for rest. Only be careful with the anxious ones. I have told you what happens when they find solid ground. If he ever carries his fear to the Enemy instead of to the screen, he will find a Kingdom that has never once been in danger, and he will sleep like a child.

Julie
Now, a word about adding. You cannot take the patient’s faith away from him, so do not try. Add to it instead.
Faith and a rule. Faith and a feeling. Faith and a special way of doing things. Faith and one more thing to buy. Let him believe the extra thing is part of the faith. He will hold on to both. He will never notice when the extra thing starts doing the holding.
While you are at it, fill his hands. Let him collect things. Things are pleasant, and there is always one more. Let him give too, but only a tenth, like a tax. Let him feel generous for it. Ten percent is the ceiling of his imagination. A man whose hands are full cannot hold them open. The Enemy wants His children to hold everything loosely. Everything they have is their Father’s, and there is always more where it came from.
But be careful. Some patients love too easily for any of this to last. They love the little children. They love the church. They would rather be in a crowded living room than anywhere else on earth. Love like that is very hard to spoil. The best you can do is keep their hands too full to open. Even then, I would not bet on it.
Now, his church.
Let him think of church as a place he goes, never a people he is. Let church mean the building and the service and the schedule. Let fellowship mean the meal afterward. You cannot keep him from having Christian friends, since the Enemy insists on it, but you can shape what those friendships are for. Steer them toward the comfortable: meals that are only meals, conversations about sports and work and the children’s activities, group texts about logistics. If they must discuss spiritual things, steer them toward complaint: about the culture, about other churches, about the direction of things. Complaint feels like discernment. It is not. Discernment costs the person doing it. Complaint costs nothing and produces the same feeling of righteousness.
Keep him busy. Not too busy, because exhausted patients sometimes break down and cry out, which is a risk we cannot run. Comfortably busy, so that he never has an hour of unstructured silence. Certain church activities are ideal. A man on three committees and who attends two Bible studies has almost no room left in which the Enemy might reach him directly. He will feel he is doing so much for the Enemy that he cannot understand why he feels so dry.
The dryness is our masterwork, but understand why. It is not the dryness itself that serves us; it is what he makes of it. Teach him to measure the Enemy by the temperature of his own heart. When he feels dry, let him conclude the Enemy has withdrawn: a season, an attack, his own inadequacy. When he feels warm, let him conclude the Enemy has drawn near. He will take his own temperature every week and call it the Enemy’s, and he will never suspect the real cause, which is that he has replaced the Enemy with activity done in the Enemy’s name.
And if you are given one of the quiet ones, a patient who does not feel much and does not say much, the same trick works in reverse. Let them watch the others weep through every song and conclude that because they feel so little, they must be far from the Enemy. They may be the nearest one in the room. See that they never find out.
Understand what all of this is guarding against. It’s easy to look at all the shrines and monuments we’ve built and get the wrong idea about whose they are. The Enemy’s idea of a church is not a building, and it is not a club. It is love with a body on it. His Son took a body once, and He has been taking bodies ever since: the bodies of ordinary people loving one another at kitchen tables and in hospital rooms and on each other’s porches, with no program, no budget, and no one telling them to. I have seen a handful of the Enemy’s people gather in a living room with nothing but a guitar and an open Book, and I tell you honestly, Grubthorn, there is nothing in all creation I find more disgusting.
If you cannot stop that kind of love, keep it lonely. There is usually one in every room who serves while the others visit: the one in the kitchen, the one clearing the plates, the one who quietly gave up something of their own so the rest could have. Let them serve everyone and be served by no one. Let them be so dependable that nobody thinks to ask how they are. Let them hear the laughter from the next room and never be pulled into it. Love that is never loved back grows tired, and tired love is something we can work with. Whatever you do, do not let the others notice. If one of them ever takes the dish towel out of their hands and sits them down at the table, you will lose the whole room at once.

Jeremiah
Let me confess something I have confessed to very few. I do not understand how the Enemy gets anything out of them without pressure. I have studied it for centuries. He threatens nothing. He demands nothing. And somehow they begin to give, and to serve, and to love their own enemies, without being told and without even seeming to try. The Enemy’s Son compared it to a branch on a vine, which is exactly the sort of thing He would say, and I have never made sense of it.
I do not need to understand it to prevent it, and neither do you. He accepted that he was saved with nothing required; he could hardly avoid it, since that is how he got onto the bridge in the first place. Now let him believe that from here on, the Enemy’s love runs on his performance: that he is welcomed on the days he does well and merely tolerated on the days he does not. He must never discover that he is loved, today, with nothing required. If that ever sinks in, love will start coming out of him on its own, and there is no pressure you can apply to stop a thing that was never driven by pressure.
Which brings me to the other kind.
Every so often a patient escapes all of this. He sees through the committees. He sees through the worship of doctrine. He hears, and I will not pretend otherwise, that the yoke is off and that the whole thing is grace. He leaves the building, and he ends up in a living room with a handful of others, eating with them and listening for the Voice.
I tell you, do not despair of him either. I have a great many patients in living rooms.
The same methods work, turned inside out. He has escaped one tribe; give him another. Let him be quietly proud of having no committees. Let him become a connoisseur of grace, able to explain it better than any preacher alive, while going years without being gracious to his own brother. Let grace itself become his new doctrine, and let him guard it as jealously as the men he left behind guarded theirs. He will not notice. They never do.
And if all else fails, turn his freedom into a chair. He has learned that his works cannot save him and that love cannot be manufactured, and curse it, he is right. Take that truth and whisper it back to him, kindly, until it becomes a reason never to move at all. “Sit on your hands,” you will tell him. “You wouldn’t want to be manufacturing works, would you?” Let him treat every plan as a program and every gathering someone organizes as a work to be questioned. Give him lofty reasons for doing nothing. He is waiting for a clearer word. He is resting in grace. He does not want to run ahead of the Spirit. He will sit so still, for so long, so as not to be mistaken for a Pharisee, that he will become a monument to nothing at all.
Rest, Grubthorn, is the Enemy’s gift. Paralysis is ours. Keep him from learning the difference.

Eli
Some patients are too serious to be kept shallow. Let those go deep, into the text, as deep as they like, provided it never becomes a conversation.
Picture a son who has memorized every letter his father ever wrote him. He can quote them. He can argue their finer points with his brothers, and he does, constantly. He has built a house out of those letters and lives in it. Meanwhile his father sits in the next room, waiting to be spoken to, and the son has not walked down the hall in years. That is the patient you want.
This is why his prayer must remain a speech. Let it be a list of requests: brief, habitual, performed in postures he no longer notices. Prayer that is only speaking is safe for us. Prayer that becomes listening is where we lose them. The Shepherd’s sheep, we are told, know His voice; so never let him get quiet enough to hear it. If he begins to sit in silence and wait, sound every alarm. Give him an itch, a memory, a task he forgot, a verse to look up. Once a patient has heard the Voice for himself, the letters stop being a house he hides in and become his Father’s handwriting, and he is VERY difficult to recover.
Finally, a word about the little screen in his pocket, because young tempters misunderstand it more than anything else.
It is not ours. It is a tool, and the Enemy’s people have a sickening habit of using it well. I have watched a living room full of them use those screens to share scripture and make themselves available to one another. A message at midnight, and someone gets in the car. A need mentioned at breakfast is met by supper. A man in a hospital bed is never alone for an hour. It is disgusting.
So do not try to take it from him. There is a right way to hold the thing and a wrong way, and our whole interest is in his grip. A man can hold the screen, or the screen can hold him. The one who holds it is available to everyone through it. The one it holds is available to it, and to no one else.
When I was young, keeping a patient from silence took real work: committees, noise, worry, the radio left on in the kitchen. Now we only need to loosen his grip until the thing is holding him, and after that it does not matter what he watches. You will be tempted to fill it with filth, and you may, but you needn’t bother. A man fixing a porch, a dog, a clip of a preacher, a clip of a war, a recipe, an outrage, another preacher. The little videos, the reels and the shorts and whatever the humans are calling them this year, do not need to be wicked. They only need to never end.
Let him reach for it before he is fully awake, before the Enemy can get a word in. Let him fall asleep with its light on his face. Let it fill every gap in his day: the line at the store, the red light, the three minutes before the service begins. The Voice has always preferred to speak into silence, and we have found a way to make sure he never has thirty seconds of it.
Most patients can be kept wide, and the screen makes it effortless. Give him a thousand sermons, none of them longer than a minute, each one satisfying, none of them digested. Every hour he spends consuming content about following the Enemy is an hour not spent following Him, and yet it produces in him the delicious sensation of spiritual seriousness. He will feel he is going deeper. He is only going wider, and wide is safe. Wide is where we keep them.
Held that way, Facebook becomes the place where his tribe gathers. There he can argue about the poor with strangers he will never meet, while the poor man on his own street goes unnoticed. There he can share a verse with four hundred people and not say a word to his Father all day. There he can type “praying for you” under a stranger’s bad news and feel that he has prayed. And there he can scroll past the brightest moments of everyone else’s life and conclude, depending on the day, that he is far behind them or far above them. Despair or pride, nephew. We are fond of both.
Best of all, he will never change his mind about it. You cannot keep his old appetites alive forever; understanding will starve them, as I told you. So give him a new appetite that does not look like sin at all. Nobody lies awake troubled over an evening of reels. He will never give it a second thought, and a man cannot change his mind about a thing he never thinks about. Nothing starves understanding like a thumb that never stops moving.
One more patient before I close, and he is the one I would most like to have. Every so often a man escapes the cage entirely. He was a caged animal once, and he knows it, and he will never go back. Do not try to put him back in. Only keep him afraid of it. Let him be afraid of standing even next to the cage. Let him see bars in every plan and a jailer in every outstretched hand. Let him defend grace with a sword when a gentle word would do, and let him answer an honest question with a verse drawn like a blade. He will win every argument. He will also wound the very brothers he is fighting for in his zeal. The Son once told a friend who drew a sword in His defense to put it away. He did not need defending then, and He does not need it now. Below help us if this man ever learns that the gospel does not need his sword. He might put it down, and then people would finally see what he has been guarding all along.
If you attend to all of this, your patient will live a saved life but a wasted one. Do not imagine he will be idle. He will do a great deal. He will serve on his committees and attend his studies and give his ten percent and win his arguments and defend his side, and whenever his hands are empty for a moment, the phone will be in them. His hands will be full every day of his life.
And when he stands before the Enemy at the end, and the work of his life is tested by fire, all of it will burn: the committees, the arguments, the flags, the right answers. Wood, hay, and stubble, every piece of it the work of his own hands. He will be saved, for we cannot prevent it, but saved as through fire, with nothing to show for it.
Better still, his children will be watching. They will inherit his pew, his committees, his opinions, and his right answers, and never once meet the Enemy. And their children will look at all of it and walk away. Who could blame them?
He will feel, on Sunday mornings, a mild warmth that he will call the presence of the Enemy. It will not be the Enemy. It will be you, Grubthorn, doing your work.
One last thing. Should he ever sit in a room and hear a letter like this one read aloud, do not be alarmed. He will find it deeply convicting, and he will think at once of several other people in the room.
And if he does not, if for once he thinks of himself, do not panic even then. Let him consider all of it in the moment. Let him be moved. Let him mean every word of it on the drive home. Tomorrow is a new day. The alarm will go off, the phone will be in his hand before his feet touch the floor, and by lunch this letter will be something he heard once, somewhere, that was very good.
Write to me again when he joins another committee. Or, if he is the other kind, when he refuses to join anything at all.
Your affectionate uncle,
Wormtongue
