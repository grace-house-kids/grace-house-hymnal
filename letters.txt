If Love Was a Rule
If Love Was a Rule
[Date: From the before]
If love were a rule, I could only ever truly love myself. 
If love were a rule, people would stop being people and start being an occasion.
If love were a rule, I would be loving the version of me who loved Him.
If love were a rule, I would resent the person who cost me more of it. 
If love were a rule, every hard person in my life would be a bill to pay.
If love were a rule, I could never actually see you. 
But love is not a rule. 
Love is what spills out of a person who has finally seen how loved they are.
That person can look up from the ledger because the ledger is closed. 
Paid. 
Nailed to a cross. 
That person can love you because they’re no longer trying to save themselves by doing it.
The Law died so that love could live.

The Height of a Coffin
The Height of a Coffin
[Date: From the before]
There is a country whose light falls without shadow because it is everywhere at once, and everything in that country is as it is because there is nothing there that can hide. And through that country runs a road, and on the road stands a man.
He came through a gate. He remembers standing before it and seeing that it was the width of a needle's eye and the height of a coffin and that he could not fit through it. 
Now he stands on a road and looks back through the gate.
The gate from this side is not what it was from the other. From the other side it had been terrible, impassable. From this side it is beautiful. And through it he can see the country he came from, and far off, he can see his brother.
His brother is standing in a field and around him are papers. Some are rolled in satchels and some in his hands as he walks in slow, deliberate circles. He is making a map. He has been for years. In the corner of that map, drawn with great care, is a gate. It is a fine gate, hidden enough and broad enough for a man of his intellect and stature to walk through upright.
But it is not the gate he is looking for. That gate is behind him, small and low, and he has never turned to face it, because when he first came into the field and glimpsed it out of the corner of his eye he registered at once that no man could fit through such a passage.The true gate, he reasoned, must lie elsewhere. 
The brother crosses paths with another man who carries a map as well. He has many men following him. He is a master cartographer. But it is a different map, drawn on different paper. 
The two men see each other and stop. They greet each other cordially but as they compare their maps, the men begin to argue. They argue about the meaning of certain hills. They argue about what the ancient descriptions of the gate should be taken to imply about its dimensions. They argue about whether the gate is more likely to be found at the end of a difficult path or at the end of an obvious one. 
They will argue in the valley for a long time. Perhaps forever. Neither of their maps leads anywhere. Both were drawn at a distance from the only gate, by men who saw that gate and turned away because they could not accept its dimensions. 
The maps are elaborate. They seem internally consistent. They are the work of intelligent and sincere men. And they are drawings of nothing. The country they are meant to guide toward does not lie at the end of either route, because the country cannot be reached by any route, only by a gate that neither map contains, and the gate is behind both of them, in a field one of them left hours ago and the other left years ago, and neither is going back to.
The man on the road, in the country of unshadowed light, watches all of this and he turns from the gate. He does not turn away in the sense of abandoning his brother. He turns because there is a road, and the road goes somewhere, and he is being drawn along it by the light that is everywhere in that country, and he walks.
Behind him, in the valley, the two men are still arguing. They will construct, between them, over the years, an entire literature of the argument. They will write treatises defending their respective maps. They will develop schools of thought around them. They will train students in their methods, and their students will refine the maps further, and further schools will develop, and further arguments, and libraries will be built to house the treatises, and the libraries will be grand, and inside them scholars will pursue the location of the gate with tremendous rigor and sincerity, but none of them will find it.
The gate is small. It is low. It is the width of a needle's eye and the height of a coffin.

The Red String
The Red String
[Date: From the before]
Two boys stood on a rusty steel platform somewhere deep in Europe. Glistening black boots sloshed through the watery snow in cadence as soldiers maintained steady order. White clouds gave their barking orders a type of power that couldn’t be easily forgotten. Two boys standing ominously against a bleak backdrop. Two boys whose tattered coats swallowed their thin frames, their shoes long worn past their last mile. But around the arm of one was something peculiar. Something that looks like defiance and yet something so innocent. A thin red string tied curiously around the boy's arm. He guarded it as if it were his only possession.

His bony fingers reached into something once resembling a pocket. A cord of scarlet revealed in the palm of a small hand, gnarled from something other than age. He pressed it towards the other boy.

"Take it. Tie it around your arm."

But the other boy barely considered it. He was somewhere else. He was deep in the small dark room of himself, arranging sentences and running calculations. He recalled that his father knew a man in the city once. He considered that his uncle carried papers once. He spoke three languages. Surely he can be useful, he thought. He could be quiet. He worked hard. His dad was a carpenter, they need carpenters, don't they? He was sanding these sentences smooth all last night and into the early hours of dawn. He looked at the string and shook his head. He refused to trust a magic talisman.

The rails began to hum as the low sound of iron grinding against iron arrived. The train came to rest, breathing its own white cloud into the bitter frost.

The soldiers moved along the line, unhurried, as if they had all the winter in the world. A woman four bodies down was holding out papers. A stamped page, folded once. She was speaking quickly and quietly, the way people speak when they believe that if they get the words in the correct order the words would hold. She is taken.

An old man clasped his hands as if in prayer, but he was not praying… he was negotiating. He named a factory. He named a foreman. He named his trade. Taken.

The boy's father knew a man. He spoke three languages. He was basically a carpenter. He revised. He corrected. He adjusted the opening. He decided he would start with their language, that would land first. That would earn him a moment. That would buy him the sentence he needed.

He couldn’t help but notice the boy with the red string. The boy was scared but had a certainty about him. Still offering that silly string. The boy wouldn’t consider it. There was no room in him for anything but his own mouth.

He was still working through his accent when the boots stopped in front of him.
He opened his mouth.

They took him.

His feet fought against the cold metal as they forced him onto the train. Bodies pressed in on him from all sides. The bellow from the train carried the sound of weeping and steam. He wiped at a strip of frosted glass with what little sleeve his coat had left. Through the streak he could see the platform and he could see the boy with the red string. He was still standing exactly where they had been standing together. He could see the soldiers had moved along, walking past him as if the space he stood in were empty air.

In his trembling hand he held up the string. He was not offering it. Not anymore. The gesture had changed. His disposition had changed. The palm was still open but it had stopped asking him to take it. It seemed to be asking one question now, "why?"
Steam hissed through the pleading as the platform began to slide away. The boy grew smaller. 

The string, still lifted. Smaller. Smaller.

He had no words left. 

Nothing left but his breath.

With every fading thought, the fog crept back onto the glass, sealing him in his tomb. 

The train whistled into the dark.

The years grew long and the war ended.

An old man buys bread at a market in a country far from where he grew up. He has come here every week for longer than the boy at the stall has been alive. The boy has known him for years without knowing him. Today his sleeve is pushed up, and the boy sees, for the first time, the two red strings on his wrist. One tied. The other threaded through the first, coiled and uncoiled.
"Why the string?" the boy asks.

The old man looks at his wrist and stays quiet for a long time. Then he tells the boy about a platform, and a train, and a friend who spoke three languages.

He tells him that a man neither he nor his friend had ever met had paid for both strings. Paid before that winter. He tells the boy how it had cost the man everything to purchase them. That any of them found wearing it would be passed over.

"The string was not a charm," he says. "It was a receipt. I didn't buy it, I could never afford something like this. All I was told to do was put it on."
The boy looks down at the second string.

The old man closes his hand around it.

"This one was his. He would have liked the color."

If you're honest…somewhere in you, you recognize this platform.

You have stood here. Maybe you are standing here now. You have felt the rails begin to hum. You have had your case ready: all the names, all the reasons, the languages, the proof of your value, the argument you built in the dark that you’re certain is true, everything you have.

And somewhere near you right now is an open hand and a scarlet cord.

It's not asking for your case. It's not interested in your reasons. Just take it.

Tie it on.

It's a gift.

And a gift like this is priceless.
